QUESTION
A human genome sequence tells us which genes exist, but it does not by itself explain how proteins work together to produce cellular functions or disease phenotypes. Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) form an interactome that links molecular components to biological outcomes. Existing interaction maps were incomplete or biased toward particular proteins and experimental contexts. Luck et al. therefore aimed to build a systematic, proteome-scale reference map of human binary PPIs that could support biological discovery across tissues and disease contexts.

METHOD
The authors expanded the human ORFeome collection to cover about 90% of protein-coding genes and screened pairwise protein combinations using nine rounds of yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening with three complementary assay versions. Candidate interactions were then verified through quadruplicate pairwise retesting and sequence confirmation. Selected interactions were also assessed with orthogonal binary assays, including MAPPIT and GPCA. The resulting network represents proteins as nodes and experimentally supported binary interactions as edges. This approach assumes that interactions detected in the assay provide useful evidence of physical protein contact, while recognizing that assay detectability is not equivalent to interaction frequency or importance in a living cell. The authors further integrated HuRI with genome, transcriptome, proteome, and tissue-expression resources to investigate functional relationships and tissue-specific interaction networks.

KEY RESULT
The HI-III-20 dataset, called HuRI, contains 52,569 verified PPIs involving 8,275 proteins. It provides substantially more high-quality binary interactions than curated small-scale literature collections and is enriched for direct physical contacts. By combining interaction data with tissue-expression information, the study inferred context-specific networks and identified interaction patterns relevant to tissue-specific functions. The authors also tested disease-associated variants and observed perturbation of interactions in examples involving Mendelian disease phenotypes, illustrating how the map can generate mechanistic hypotheses.

LIMITATION
HuRI is a reference map, not a complete human interactome: the authors estimate that it captures roughly 2–11% of binary PPIs. Y2H has limited sensitivity and can miss interactions that depend on post-translational modifications, additional binding partners, or particular cellular conditions. Screening one isoform per gene also misses interactions unique to alternative spliceforms. Therefore, absence of an edge should not be interpreted as proof that two proteins never interact, and tissue-filtered networks are inferred contexts rather than direct measurements of every interaction in native cells. HuRI is most useful as a systematic scaffold that must be integrated with complementary evidence and experimentally validated.
